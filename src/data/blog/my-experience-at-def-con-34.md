DEF CON 34 was my first DEF CON, and it was honestly one of the most memorable experiences I've had.

I've been interested in cybersecurity for a long time, especially CTFs, reverse engineering, and binary exploitation. So naturally, going to one of the largest hacker conventions in the world was something I really wanted to do.

The biggest reason I wanted to go wasn't necessarily the talks, competitions, or hacking. It was the people. DEF CON is filled with people who share the same interests as me, and I wanted to experience that in person.

I spent a week in Las Vegas with my dad, met people I'd previously only known online, competed in CTFs, reverse engineered hardware, and somehow managed to be the first person to hack a badge.

Here's how it went.

## Arriving at DEF CON

My first impression when I arrived was pretty much just "woah."

I knew DEF CON was big, but actually being there was completely different from seeing videos or hearing people talk about it online.

There were so many people interested in cybersecurity, all in one place. People from completely different backgrounds, with different skill sets and interests, all brought together by hacking.

It was honestly pretty crazy to see.

One thing I wasn't prepared for, though, was how expensive Las Vegas is. Seriously, everything costs an absurd amount of money. That was probably my biggest complaint about the entire trip.

But besides Vegas trying to bankrupt everyone who steps outside, the experience was incredible.

## Meeting People I Knew Online

This was easily my favorite part of DEF CON.

I've spent a lot of time in cybersecurity communities online, and over time I've gotten to know quite a few people. At DEF CON, I finally got to meet some of them in person.

That included people from **Shellphish** and **Hack@UCF alumni**, along with other friends I've met through cybersecurity.

It's funny because when I was younger, my parents always told me not to talk to strangers on the internet.

And now here I am, flying across the country to meet them.

The irony is pretty funny.

But seriously, getting to meet these people in person was awesome. When you're involved in cybersecurity, so much of your interaction with other people happens online, whether that's through Discord, CTF teams, or other communities.

Actually meeting them face-to-face was a completely different experience.

I think that was one of the things that made DEF CON so special to me. It wasn't just about hacking computers. It was about meeting the people behind the usernames.

## Hacking Bunny and Baochip's Badge

One of the coolest technical experiences I had at DEF CON was working with my dad to reverse engineer a hardware badge made by Bunny and Baochip.

If you're familiar with DEF CON, you probably know that badges are a pretty big part of the culture. They're not always just something you wear around your neck. A lot of them are actual pieces of hardware that people can explore, reverse engineer, and hack.

My dad and I spent time reverse engineering the hardware to figure out how it worked.

And we managed to hack it.

The best part?

**We were the first people to hack it, and Bunny himself confirmed it.**

That was honestly pretty awesome.

Being able to work on hardware reverse engineering with my dad at DEF CON, and then finding out we were the first to successfully hack the badge, was definitely one of the highlights of the trip.

My dad also thought the whole experience was extremely fun, which made it even better.

It's one thing to work on something technical by yourself, but being able to share that experience with someone else makes it a lot more memorable.

## CTFs, CTFs, and More CTFs

Naturally, I spent a pretty significant amount of time doing CTFs.

For anyone unfamiliar, CTF stands for Capture the Flag. They're cybersecurity competitions where you solve challenges involving things like reverse engineering, binary exploitation, cryptography, forensics, and other areas of security.

During DEF CON, I participated in several competitions and challenges, including:

- **SnackyCTF**
- **StarpwnCTF**
- **Hack The Box (HTB) CTF**
- **Lockheed Martin Aeronautics Cyber Range**

One of my favorite accomplishments was during the Lockheed Martin Aeronautics Cyber Range.

I managed to **get first blood on a challenge**, meaning I was the first person to solve it.

The challenge involved digital forensics and steganography, which is the practice of hiding information inside other data.

Successfully solving it first earned me a badge, which was pretty cool.

I've done plenty of CTFs online, but getting to participate in them at DEF CON was a different experience.

There's something about being surrounded by other people doing the exact same thing that makes it more enjoyable.

## Exploring the Villages

DEF CON has a ton of different villages, each focusing on a particular area of cybersecurity.

I spent most of my time in three:

### CTF Village

This was probably one of the most obvious places for me to spend time.

I already spend a lot of time playing CTFs, so naturally, I gravitated toward the CTF Village.

Being able to participate in challenges and spend time around other people interested in competitive hacking was awesome.

### Red Team Village

The Red Team Village was another area I spent a lot of time exploring.

Red teaming is an area of cybersecurity that I'm really interested in, so it was cool getting to experience a village dedicated to it.

### Game Hacking Village

I also spent time at the Game Hacking Village.

Game hacking is a pretty interesting area because it brings together reverse engineering, software security, and games.

Considering my interests, it was another place I naturally ended up spending time.

### The Problem With Spending Too Much Time in One Place

Here's something I realized afterward.

**I spent way too much time in the same few villages.**

Don't get me wrong, I had a ton of fun. But DEF CON has so much more to offer than just the three areas I spent most of my time in.

There were plenty of other villages and activities I didn't get to explore as much as I would've liked.

And that's probably my biggest regret from the convention.

I got so focused on the things I already enjoyed that I didn't spend enough time discovering new things.

Which, looking back, kind of defeats part of the purpose of going to an event with so many different areas of cybersecurity.

## What I Learned

One technical area I got more interested in during DEF CON was hash cracking.

But I think the biggest thing I learned wasn't necessarily technical.

It was about the cybersecurity community itself.

One of the things that stood out to me was just how diverse the community is.

There are people with completely different backgrounds, experiences, interests, and skill levels. Some focus on hardware, others on software, some on offensive security, and others on completely different areas.

And that's something I think people need to embrace.

You don't have to be interested in the same things as everyone else, and you definitely don't have to approach cybersecurity the same way.

There's so much to learn from people who think differently or specialize in things you might know absolutely nothing about.

DEF CON really made that clear to me.

## My Advice for First-Time Attendees

If you're interested in cybersecurity and have the opportunity to attend DEF CON, I'd absolutely recommend going.

Especially if you're younger and already involved in the cybersecurity community.

But I have one major piece of advice:

**Don't get attached to one thing.**

Seriously.

I spent most of my time in the CTF Village, Red Team Village, and Game Hacking Village because those were the areas I was already interested in.

And while I had an awesome time, I definitely missed out on exploring other parts of the convention.

There's so much happening at DEF CON that you really can't experience everything, but you should at least make an effort to explore areas outside your usual interests.

You might discover something you didn't even know you were interested in.

Also, if you know people in the cybersecurity community who are attending, take the opportunity to meet them.

For me, meeting people was the best part of the entire trip.

And finally, prepare yourself for Las Vegas prices. Because apparently charging ridiculous amounts of money for everything is part of the experience.

## Will I Be Back for DEF CON 35?

Absolutely.

I'm already planning on attending DEF CON 35.

Next time, though, I'm going to make a much bigger effort to explore more of the villages and experience parts of the convention I missed this year.

I'll obviously still be doing CTFs, because there's no way I'm going to DEF CON and not doing those.

But I also want to branch out more.

## Final Thoughts

Looking back, DEF CON 34 was an incredible experience.

I got to spend a week in Las Vegas with my dad, meet people I'd known online, participate in multiple CTFs, get first blood on a Lockheed Martin challenge, and work with my dad to become the first people to hack Bunny and Baochip's badge.

But even with all of that, my favorite part was still meeting people.

Cybersecurity is something I've spent a huge amount of time learning and working on, and being surrounded by people who share that interest was an awesome experience.

It also reminded me that cybersecurity isn't just about finding vulnerabilities, solving challenges, or writing exploits.

It's also about the community.

And DEF CON has one hell of a community.

**See you at DEF CON 35.**
